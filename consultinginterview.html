The Consulting Interview

Eleven decks of business material are behind you, which means the harder half of the preparation is already done. This last one is about the other half: the day itself, the questions about you rather than the client, and everything that happens between hearing a prompt and someone making a decision. Nothing here will tell you what your stories should be, because those are yours and interviewers can tell when they are not. What it does cover is what each question is really testing, how a strong answer is shaped, and how to stay steady when a number comes out wrong or a question lands somewhere you had not planned for.

Card 1 of 13

You get an invitation to a first round at a consulting firm and the email just says two interviews, forty-five minutes each. What does a full recruiting process actually look like, and what is each round for?

First rounds test whether you can do the work, final rounds test whether partners want you doing it.

A first round is usually two or three interviews with consultants and managers, each split between questions about you and a case. Clear that and you go to a final round, often on a single day, with three or four more interviews weighted toward partners, sometimes plus a written or group exercise. The material is similar throughout, but the emphasis shifts: early rounds lean on structure and numbers, later ones on judgment and whether a partner would put you in a client room. The half about you is scored just as carefully as the case, in every round.

Card 2 of 13

“Why consulting?” It comes up in almost every fit interview, and everyone has an answer ready. What does a credible one actually contain, and what makes an answer sound hollow?

Name what the work genuinely involves, then point at something in your own history that fits it.

The interviewer is checking whether you know what the job is day to day, not whether you can compliment the industry. Credible answers name concrete features of the work: a new problem every few months, small teams, forming a defensible view fast on incomplete information, and a steep first year. Then they tie at least one of those to something you have already done and liked. Hollow answers stay generic, since wanting to solve hard problems with clever people describes almost any graduate job. Saying what you want out of it is fine, as long as that is not only the exit options.

Card 3 of 13

Two firms in the same fortnight will both ask “why us?” How do you make that answer specific without it sliding into flattery?

Specificity comes from people you have spoken to and work you have looked at, not adjectives about the firm.

Every firm’s website says roughly the same things, so the interviewer is looking for evidence you went past it. The material that works is first-hand: what people at the firm told you when you spoke to them, a practice or office that matches what you want to do, how staffing and early responsibility actually run there. Naming a real difference and saying why it matters to you beats praise, and telling someone their firm is simply the best is both unfalsifiable and a little awkward to receive. It is also perfectly honest to say the firms overlap a lot and here is the specific reason you would pick this one.

Card 4 of 13

Some firms call it the fit interview, another calls it the personal experience interview. Underneath the different names, what is that half of the conversation really probing?

It probes a few recurring qualities, which is why four or five real experiences cover most of the questions.

The wording varies but the dimensions repeat: whether you can move a group toward something, whether you push yourself past comfortable, whether you handle friction with another person honestly, and whether you can see your own part in something that went badly. Because those overlap, one experience told with a different emphasis can legitimately answer several questions, which is why preparing a small set you know deeply beats drafting an answer per prompt. Choose situations you were genuinely central to, since the follow-ups go several layers past what you first say and borrowed credit falls apart quickly there.

Card 5 of 13

You are two minutes into describing a project you ran and the interviewer has not written anything down. How should a fit answer be shaped so the result lands, without it sounding memorised?

Short setup, most of your airtime on what you decided and why, then one result you can actually name.

Consulting fit interviews go deep on one experience rather than skimming several, so a long scene-setting preamble spends the time you needed for the part being assessed. Give the situation in two sentences, then talk about what you personally decided and the reasoning behind each decision, because the reasoning is what gets probed. Finish on something concrete enough to picture: a number, a decision that got made, a person who changed their mind. Then stop, since the follow-up questions are where most of the assessment happens. Rehearsing the shape is sensible, rehearsing the sentences is what makes an answer sound canned.

Card 6 of 13

“There are five minutes left, what questions do you have?” What separates a question that helps you from one that just fills the silence?

Ask something only this particular person could answer, and genuinely want to hear the answer.

The moment is part courtesy and part a last read on whether you are curious about the work. A question is strong when it is addressed to that person’s own experience: how a team handled a client who disagreed with the answer, what surprised them in their first year. It is weak when the firm’s website answers it, or when it is really a statement about how keen you are, dressed up with a question mark. Hours and pay matter, but a recruiter is a better place to raise them than a first-round interview. This tends to be the last thing that happens, so people remember it.

Card 7 of 13

“Tell me about a time you led a team.” You have never held a job title with the word lead in it. What does an interviewer actually mean by leadership here?

Leadership means you changed what a group did, whether or not you had any authority over them.

Titles are not the point. What counts is evidence that a group did something it would not have done without you: you saw what needed doing, you brought other people with you, and you carried some of the consequence either way. A society, a sports team, a group project where one person was not delivering all qualify. The probing lands on what you did about the obstacle or the difficult person, so a story where everything went smoothly leaves you with nothing to say. Consulting teams are three or four people and juniors own a piece of the work early, which is why this question is so consistent.

Card 8 of 13

A company sells 1.8 million units a year at $23 each and you need revenue, with no calculator on the table. How should arithmetic under pressure actually sound?

Round to friendly numbers, carry the zeros as units, and say the sanity check out loud before you commit.

Say what you are about to do before you do it, then round deliberately and keep track of which way you rounded: 1.8 million times 20 is 36 million, plus 1.8 million times 3 is 5.4 million, so about 41 million. Work in units, thousands or millions, rather than counting zeros, since dropped zeros cause more wrong answers than hard multiplication does. Before you commit, check the size against something you know, so a figure implying every household spends a thousand dollars gets caught by you rather than by the interviewer. Narrate all of it, because silent arithmetic earns no credit and a slip caught early costs almost nothing.

Card 9 of 13

“Tell me about a time you convinced someone to change their mind.” It is close to the most-asked question in consulting fit interviews. Why that one, and what makes an answer strong?

Persuasion is scored on whether you understood the other person’s reasons, not on whether you won.

Nearly all consulting work ends with someone who does not report to you choosing to do something differently, often someone more senior with more context than you have. The question is a direct proxy for that. Strong answers spend real time on why the other person held their position, since understanding it is the part that separates persuading from repeating yourself more loudly, and then on what specifically shifted: evidence you brought, a concern you addressed, a version of the plan you changed. Wearing someone down until they gave in reads poorly. Moving them only partway is fine, as long as you can say what moved and why.

Card 10 of 13

“Tell me about a piece of feedback you found hard to hear.” Why does the interviewer care far more about what happened next than about the feedback itself?

The test is whether you can hear something unwelcome, take the real point, and change something because of it.

Consulting runs on frequent and fairly blunt feedback, given upward as well as downward, and someone who cannot absorb it gets expensive to manage quickly. So state the feedback plainly rather than softening it into a disguised compliment, be honest about your first reaction, then spend most of the answer on what you did differently afterwards and how you knew it worked. Picking something so trivial that nothing was at stake is the usual failure, closely followed by concluding the feedback was wrong. Conflict questions work the same way: name the disagreement, show you understood the other side, and describe what you did rather than how you felt about it.

Card 11 of 13

Your final round includes a written case: forty minutes with a pack of exhibits, then a short presentation. How is that different from a live case, and what does a group case add on top?

Nobody feeds you the data in a written case, so time budgeting and a clear first page do most of the work.

A live case is a conversation where information arrives when you ask for it. In a written case you get everything at once, much of it irrelevant, and nobody tells you which parts matter, so budget the time before you start reading: a quick skim to fix the question, the bulk on the two or three exhibits that bear on it, and a hard stop to build and rehearse. Lead the first page with the recommendation, not the background. Group cases add a second thing being assessed, whether you make the group better, so drawing in someone quiet or reconciling two views counts for more than holding the floor.

Card 12 of 13

You have just given a market size of four billion dollars and the interviewer pauses and asks whether you are sure. What do you do in the next thirty seconds?

Go back to the step you trust least, fix it out loud, and move on without apologising three times.

Treat the question as information rather than a verdict, because sometimes it flags a genuine slip and sometimes it tests whether you will abandon a defensible answer under mild pressure. Retrace briefly and out loud from the assumption or the arithmetic step you are least confident in, rather than restarting the whole calculation from scratch. If it is wrong, say what it should be and carry on in a sentence. If you still think it is right, say calmly why. What actually costs people offers is the spiral afterwards: repeated apologies, a lost thread, and five worse minutes. Errors are normal, unrecoverable errors are not.

Card 13 of 13

Two candidates reach the same recommendation on the same case and only one of them gets an offer. What actually separates them, and why does the other one rarely find out?

The final answer is seldom the differentiator, how you got there and what you were like usually is.

A case is scored on several things at once, and the recommendation is only one: whether the structure was genuinely yours, whether you drove the case or waited to be led, and whether the numbers were handled cleanly. Alongside that sits a quieter judgment: would they put you in a client meeting in six months, and want you there at the end of a long week. The candidate who says less but says it clearly, listens to a follow-up, and stays pleasant when a question goes badly usually wins. None of it comes back as feedback, which is why it is worth deciding in advance how you want to come across.
