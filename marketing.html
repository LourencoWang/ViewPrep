Marketing Fundamentals

Marketing questions show up in more interviews than people expect, not just marketing roles, but general business and case interviews that touch product or strategy. This deck covers the core vocabulary and frameworks, like the four Ps, that give you a structured way to talk about how a product actually gets positioned, priced, and sold. Written for someone starting from zero, with an emphasis on frameworks you can actually apply to a real example on the spot.

Card 1 of 14

A client's CFO tells you he wants to cut the marketing budget, and points straight at the ad spend. What would you tell him marketing actually covers?

Marketing is the whole job of matching a product to people who want it, and ads are only the last mile.

Marketing is the work of figuring out which customers to serve, what those customers actually need, and then designing, pricing and distributing something that meets that need before a single ad runs. Advertising is just the visible end of it, the part that tells people the thing exists. Deciding to sell a smaller pack at a lower price to a different kind of shopper is a marketing decision with no advertising in it at all. That matters in a case, because a problem labelled marketing very often turns out to be about product or price, not promotion.

Card 2 of 14

What is market segmentation, and what are some common bases for it?

Segmentation splits one big, messy market into smaller groups that actually behave the same way.

Market segmentation is the process of breaking a broad customer base into smaller groups that share similar characteristics or needs, so a company, or the team advising it, can market to each group more precisely than it could to everyone at once. Common bases include demographic (age, income), geographic (location), psychographic (lifestyle, values), and behavioral (purchase habits, brand loyalty).

Card 3 of 14

A regional eyewear retailer is expanding into a new market and isn't sure which customers to go after first or how to stand out once it's there. What three-step process would you walk the client through?

STP walks you from a broad, messy market down to one clear position in customers' minds.

Walk the firm through STP: Segmentation, Targeting, and Positioning. Segmentation splits the new market into groups that share similar needs, targeting picks which of those groups is actually worth pursuing first, and positioning defines how the firm should be perceived, relative to competitors, in that group's mind. Run through in order, it takes the client from 'everyone in this new market' to a specific, ownable position for a specific audience.

Card 4 of 14

A retailer is about to launch a new private-label snack line and wants a structured way to plan it. What framework would you reach for, and what does it cover?

The 4 Ps are the four levers a company pulls to bring a product to market.

Reach for the 4 Ps, or marketing mix: Product (what you're selling), Price (what it costs), Place (where and how customers can get it), and Promotion (how you tell people about it). For the retailer's snack launch, that means nailing down what the product actually is, what it's priced at, which shelves or channels it lands in, and how shoppers hear about it. Every go-to-market decision falls under one of these four buckets, and getting them working together is what makes a launch land instead of falling flat.

Card 5 of 14

A food manufacturer sells its sauces through supermarkets, and now wants to sell direct from its own website. The supermarket buyer is furious. What is going on here, and how would you frame the decision?

Channel conflict is what happens when two routes to the same customer start competing with each other.

A distribution channel is the path a product travels to reach the buyer, either direct from the company or indirectly through wholesalers and retailers. Selling direct earns the food manufacturer the full margin and gives it customer data it never sees from a supermarket, but the supermarket delivers volume and shelf space the food manufacturer cannot replicate on its own. Channel conflict is the clash that follows, especially if the website undercuts the shelf price and the retailer responds by cutting orders or delisting. The decision comes down to whether the extra direct margin outweighs the volume the client is putting at risk.

Card 6 of 14

What's the difference between a push strategy and a pull strategy, and when would each one make sense?

Push sells the product into the channel, pull makes customers walk in and ask the channel for it.

A push strategy aims your effort at the middle of the chain, using trade discounts, sales reps and shelf incentives so wholesalers and retailers stock the product and move it. A pull strategy aims past them at the end customer, using advertising, samples and word of mouth so people come asking for it by name, which forces retailers to carry it. A new brand nobody has heard of usually has to push first, while a brand people already trust can afford to pull. Most real programs run both at once, and the interesting question is the mix.

Card 7 of 14

What's the difference between B2B and B2C marketing?

B2B sells to a committee on ROI; B2C sells to one person on emotion and price.

B2B (business-to-business) marketing sells to other companies, where multiple stakeholders typically weigh in and decisions hinge on ROI over a longer sales cycle — the kind of buying committee you'd map out on a client engagement. B2C (business-to-consumer) marketing sells directly to individual consumers, where decisions are usually faster and driven more by emotion and price than by a formal evaluation process.

Card 8 of 14

What is brand equity, and why does it matter in a pricing engagement?

Brand equity is the extra value a name adds on top of what the product actually does.

Brand equity is the value a brand name adds beyond the product's raw functional benefits: the premium customers will pay, the loyalty they show, and the trust they extend before they've even used it. It's often the first thing to check when a client's product and a competitor's are functionally identical but priced far apart — two nearly identical products can sell at very different prices purely because one has a name behind it that people already believe in.

Card 9 of 14

A haulage company Fitness is spending heavily on ads and getting plenty of website traffic, but sales are flat. How would you use a funnel to work out where the money is leaking?

A funnel turns a vague ‘sales are flat’ into one specific stage where conversion is falling short.

A funnel counts how many people survive each step on the way to buying, so you can read the conversion rate between steps rather than the total at the end. Say 200,000 people see the ad, 20,000 visit the site, 6,000 start checkout and 600 buy: the ad to visit step converts at 10 percent and checkout to purchase at 10 percent, when a working checkout usually holds 40 percent or more. That gap says the problem is at the bottom, not the top. Fixing a late step is normally cheaper than buying more traffic, because it multiplies through every stage below it.

Card 10 of 14

Vantry Appliances says its flagship blender has gone flat after five years of strong growth, and the team wants to spend more on advertising. How would you think about where that product sits in its life?

Products run through introduction, growth, maturity and decline, and the right move changes at each stage.

Products tend to follow a curve: a slow introduction while few people know it exists, a growth phase as adoption spreads, a long maturity where almost everyone who wants one has one, and eventually decline. Flat sales after five strong years usually means maturity, where the category has stopped growing and extra volume can only come from a competitor's share. Pouring more money into advertising rarely restarts a mature category, so the usual plays are defending margin, taking cost out, or extending the line with variants. You cannot observe the stage directly, so treat it as a hypothesis and test it against category growth.

Card 11 of 14

Larkspur Meals, a subscription meal-kit service, wants to know how much it can afford to spend acquiring a new subscriber and still turn a profit. What number would you calculate, and what does it capture?

CLV is the total profit one customer is worth over the whole relationship, not just their first order.

Calculate customer lifetime value: the total profit, not revenue, that Larkspur can expect from a single subscriber across the entire relationship, not just their first box. Take the contribution left after the food and delivery costs of an average box, then multiply by how often they order and how long they stay. Because it captures repeat purchases over time, CLV is the number that tells Larkspur how much it can afford to spend acquiring a customer and still come out ahead. Use revenue instead and you overstate that ceiling by the entire cost of making and shipping the boxes, which is how a business ends up losing money on every subscriber it wins.

Card 12 of 14

Larkspur Meals spent nine million dollars on marketing last year and added 150,000 subscribers. Finance says a subscriber is worth 180 dollars over their life. What do you calculate, and what does it tell you?

CAC is what you pay to win one customer, and lifetime value over CAC tells you whether it was worth paying.

Customer acquisition cost is total acquisition spend divided by the customers it won, so here nine million over 150,000 is 60 dollars a subscriber. Set that against the 180 dollar lifetime value and you get a ratio of three to one, which subscription businesses generally read as healthy: near one to one you are barely covering the cost of winning the customer, while a very high ratio can mean you are underspending and leaving growth on the table. It also matters how fast the 60 dollars comes back, since a long payback ties up cash. Cutting churn raises lifetime value and improves the ratio without spending another dollar on ads.

Card 13 of 14

A retailer runs search ads, a podcast sponsorship and an email program all at once, and sales are up 12 percent. The CMO wants to know which one worked. Why is that so hard to answer honestly?

Attribution tries to credit a sale to the marketing that caused it, and overlapping activity muddies it.

Attribution is the set of rules for splitting credit for a sale across the touchpoints a customer saw on the way to it. Last-click attribution hands all the credit to the final touch, which flatters search and email while starving the podcast that created the demand in the first place. Multi-touch models spread the credit around, but the weights are assumptions someone chose, not measurements. The honest test is an experiment: hold spend back in a set of comparable regions and compare against the rest, which is what a good interviewer wants to hear you reach for before the dashboard.

Card 14 of 14

A discount chain undercuts the firm’s mid-price eyewear by 30 percent, and the firm’s CEO wants to match. Walk me through the trade-off you'd put in front of him.

Matching a discounter buys volume today by spending down the brand equity that justified your price.

Start with the arithmetic. If a pair sells for 100 with 50 of cost in it, the firm keeps 50, and cutting the price 30 percent leaves 20, so it needs two and a half times the units just to stand still. Then the harder part: price tells customers where a brand sits, so matching resets what they believe the product is worth, and that belief is very hard to walk back up. Check too whether the discounter is even fishing in the firm’s segment. Often the better answer is defending the dimension you own, or launching a cheaper second line that takes the fight without dragging the main brand down.
