Financial Markets & Instruments

This deck covers how stocks, bonds, and other financial instruments actually trade, the difference between where a security is first issued and where it changes hands afterward, and the basic mechanics behind the markets you hear about in the news. It is aimed at giving you enough grounding to talk about markets confidently in an interview, not at making you a trader. No assumed background beyond general curiosity about how finance works.

Card 1 of 17

What does it actually mean to own a share of stock?

A share is a small ownership slice of a company, not a loan to it.

A share of stock is a unit of ownership in a company. If the food manufacturer has 50 million shares outstanding and you hold 500 of them, you own one hundred-thousandth of the business, which comes with a claim on that same fraction of its profits and, usually, a vote on matters put to shareholders. You aren’t lending the company anything and nobody promises you your money back. Your return comes from the shares being worth more later, or from dividends, cash the company chooses to pay out to its owners.

Card 2 of 17

What is a bond, and what do the words coupon, par value and maturity mean?

A bond is a loan on fixed terms, set interest payments plus a set date to get your money back.

A bond is a loan you make to a company or a government, packaged so that it can be bought and sold. The par value, also called face value, is the amount the issuer promises to repay at the end, conventionally $1,000 per bond. The coupon is the fixed interest paid along the way, so a 5% coupon on a $1,000 bond is $50 a year. Maturity is the date the last coupon and the par value come back. You get no ownership and no upside beyond those promised payments.

Card 3 of 17

What’s the difference between a primary market and a secondary market?

Primary market money goes to the company; secondary market money just moves between investors.

In the primary market, a company sells brand-new securities directly to investors for the first time, an IPO being the classic example, and the company keeps the proceeds — this is the deal-side work an ECM (equity capital markets) team executes. In the secondary market, investors simply trade those already-issued securities amongst themselves, like on the NYSE or Nasdaq, and the issuing company isn't even a party to the transaction. Only the primary market actually raises new capital for the company; the secondary market just lets ownership change hands.

Card 4 of 17

What is an IPO, exactly?

An IPO is a private company's first sale of shares to the public, becoming publicly traded.

An IPO, or initial public offering, is the process by which a private company like the retailer sells shares to the public for the first time and becomes a publicly traded company. It lets the company raise capital from a much broader pool of investors, but in exchange, existing owners give up some ownership and the company takes on the disclosure and reporting obligations that come with being public.

Card 5 of 17

Someone taps buy on their phone and the shares appear in their account. What does a broker do, and what does an exchange do?

The broker takes your order and routes it, the exchange is the venue where it meets the other side.

A broker is a firm licensed to place orders on your behalf and to hold the resulting shares and cash in your account, since an individual can’t walk onto an exchange and trade directly. The exchange is the venue itself, a regulated marketplace that collects everyone’s buy and sell orders into one order book and matches them by price. So the sequence is you instruct the broker, the broker routes the order to an exchange or another trading venue, the exchange pairs it with someone willing to take the other side, and the fill comes back to your account.

Card 6 of 17

What is the bid-ask spread?

The bid-ask spread is the gap between what buyers offer and what sellers demand.

The bid is the highest price a buyer is currently willing to pay for a security, and the ask is the lowest price a seller is willing to accept; the difference between the two is the spread a trading desk watches all day. A narrow spread usually signals a liquid market with plenty of active buyers and sellers, while a wide spread signals lower liquidity or more uncertainty about fair value.

Card 7 of 17

You buy the haulage company at the market and the fill comes back three cents worse than the price on your screen. What’s the difference between a market order and a limit order?

A market order guarantees you trade, a limit order guarantees your price, never both.

A market order says fill me now at the best price available, so it trades immediately but you accept whatever the ask happens to be at that instant, which is why the fill can drift a few cents from the last quote you saw. A limit order sets the worst price you’ll accept, buy at $49.80 or better, and it simply rests in the order book until someone trades against it or you cancel. The trade-off is certainty of execution against certainty of price, and in a thinly traded name with a wide spread that difference gets expensive fast.

Card 8 of 17

What is market capitalization, and how is it calculated?

Market cap is share price times shares outstanding, a company's total public equity value.

Market capitalization is the total value of a company's outstanding shares, calculated simply as the current share price multiplied by the number of shares outstanding. It's the standard way to categorize company size — large-cap, mid-cap, small-cap — and to compare the relative size of publicly traded companies at a glance, exactly what you'd use to screen comps for a pitch like this one.

Card 9 of 17

What is a market index, and what do people mean by active versus passive investing?

An index is a rules-based basket used as a scoreboard, and passive funds just buy the whole basket.

An index is a defined basket of securities whose combined value is tracked over time, usually weighted by market capitalization so the largest companies count for the most. You can’t buy the index itself, it’s a scoreboard for how a slice of the market performed. Active investing means a manager picks individual holdings hoping to beat that scoreboard and charges a higher fee for the attempt, while passive investing means holding a fund that simply owns the basket at very low cost. Most active managers fail to beat their index after fees over long stretches, which is why passive money has grown so quickly.

Card 10 of 17

Redhill Utilities is raising money and you can buy either its bonds or its shares. As the investor, how do the two differ in risk, return, and where you stand if the company fails?

Debt is paid first for a capped return, equity is paid last with no ceiling on the upside.

Buying the bonds makes you a lender: you get fixed coupons, your principal back at maturity, and legal priority, meaning that if Redhill is wound up, creditors are paid out of the proceeds before shareholders see anything. The most you can make is the yield you were promised. Buying the shares makes you an owner: nothing is promised, you sit last in line and can be wiped out entirely, but you’re entitled to everything left over once the lenders are satisfied, and that has no upper limit. Same company, two very different risk profiles.

Card 11 of 17

What is preferred stock, and where does it sit between debt and common stock?

Preferred stock pays a fixed dividend and ranks ahead of common shares but behind every lender.

Preferred stock is an ownership stake that behaves partly like a bond. It normally pays a fixed dividend, and in a wind-down preferred holders are paid after all lenders but before common shareholders, the ordinary shares people mean when they just say stock. In exchange for that steadier income and better place in the queue, preferred holders usually give up their vote and most of the upside if the business does well. Because it sits between debt and equity, bankers call it hybrid capital, and it shows up constantly in private equity deals and rescue financings.

Card 12 of 17

A bond’s price drops overnight and the client asks why the yield just moved. How do you explain the relationship between a bond’s yield and its price?

A bond's yield and price always move in opposite directions.

A bond's yield is the return an investor actually earns from holding it, and it moves inversely to the bond's price: when price falls, like it just did on the firm’s bond, yield rises, because the same fixed coupon payments now represent a bigger percentage return on the lower price paid. Pay less for the same future cash flows and your return goes up; pay more and it goes down. This inverse relationship is one of the most fundamental mechanics in fixed income markets.

Card 13 of 17

A new bond issue is rated one notch below investment grade. What does a credit rating tell investors, and why does it matter for pricing the deal?

Lower credit rating means higher default risk, so investors demand a higher yield.

A credit rating, assigned by agencies like Moody's or S&P, is their estimate of how likely an issuer like the firm is to default on its debt. Ratings range from investment-grade at the safer end down to high-yield, or “junk,” at the riskier end. Because risk and return are linked, a rating this low means the firm will have to offer a higher yield to compensate investors for taking on that extra default risk.

Card 14 of 17

At the morning meeting someone says the curve just inverted and the desk reacts. What is the yield curve, and why does an inverted one get that much attention?

The yield curve plots yield against maturity, and inversion means short-dated debt yields more than long.

The yield curve plots the yield on one issuer’s bonds, usually a government’s, against how long each one has until it matures, from a few months out to thirty years. Normally it slopes upward, because lending for longer leaves more time for inflation and trouble, so investors demand more to do it. An inverted curve slopes down, with short maturities yielding more than long ones, which implies the market expects interest rates, and typically growth, to be lower in future. Inversions have preceded most recent recessions, though the signal is imprecise and the lag has varied a lot.

Card 15 of 17

Rates move a quarter of a point and a thirty-year bond falls far more than a two-year. Why does maturity change how much a bond’s price moves?

The longer you wait for the money, the more a change in rates moves the bond’s price today.

A bond’s price is just the present value of its future payments, and discounting bites harder the further out a payment sits. Raise the discount rate a quarter of a point and a payment arriving in thirty years loses much more of its value than one arriving in two, so the long bond drops far more for exactly the same move in rates. That sensitivity is called duration, roughly the weighted average time until you receive your money, and it’s the standard measure of interest-rate risk on a fixed income desk. A lower coupon lengthens duration too.

Card 16 of 17

What is a derivative, and what are options and futures examples of?

A derivative's value comes from something else entirely; options and futures are the two classic examples.

A derivative is a financial contract with no value of its own; its value is derived from an underlying asset, like a stock, commodity, or index — the kind of exposure a derivatives or structuring desk trades all day. Options and futures are both derivatives, but they work differently: an option gives the holder the right, but not the obligation, to buy or sell the underlying at a set price, while a futures contract is an obligation, both sides are locked in to buy or sell it at a set price on a set future date.

Card 17 of 17

What is the difference between a call option and a put option, and how would a fund use one to protect a long position ahead of earnings?

Calls profit when the price rises above the strike; puts profit when it falls below it.

A call option gives the holder the right to buy the underlying asset, like the retailer stock, at a set strike price before expiration, and investors buy calls when they expect the price to rise. A put option gives the holder the right to sell at the strike price, which is the side the industrial target would look at to hedge against a drop, and investors buy puts when they expect the price to fall. Either way, it's a right, not an obligation, so the holder can just let the option expire worthless if exercising it wouldn't be profitable.
