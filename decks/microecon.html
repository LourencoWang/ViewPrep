Microeconomics & Market Structures

Interviewers in business, strategy, and even product roles often lean on basic economic reasoning: supply and demand, how competition shapes pricing, what happens when a market has only a few players instead of many. This deck builds those ideas up from first principles, so if the words "market structure" currently mean nothing to you, that is exactly who this is for. The goal is to be able to reason through an economic scenario out loud, not recite a textbook definition.

Card 1 of 17

Before we get to curves and prices: what is a market, and what do economists actually mean by the words supply and demand?

Demand is what buyers would buy at every price, supply is what sellers would offer at every price.

A market is any arrangement where buyers and sellers come together to trade something, whether that's a weekend produce stall, an online marketplace, or the worldwide market for copper. Demand isn't a single quantity, it's the whole picture of how much buyers would purchase at each possible price, and supply is the mirror image, how much sellers would be willing to produce and offer at each possible price. Because both are lists of price and quantity pairs, they get drawn as curves. Nearly every case that touches pricing, volume, or competition is really a question about one of those two curves.

Card 2 of 17

What does the law of demand state?

Higher prices mean people buy less; lower prices mean they buy more.

The law of demand says that, all else equal, a higher price leads people to buy less of a good, and a lower price leads them to buy more, which is what gives the demand curve its downward slope. It happens because as something gets relatively more expensive, buyers shift toward substitutes that meet the same need for less — the same substitution logic that shows up whenever a case turns on a proposed price change.

Card 3 of 17

The demand curve slopes down. Why does the supply curve slope up?

A higher price makes production worth it for more sellers, so more units get offered.

As price rises, each unit sold becomes more profitable, so existing producers add shifts and new producers decide it's worth entering, and the total quantity offered goes up. Underneath that is a cost story: the cheapest and easiest units get made first, and squeezing out additional units usually costs more per unit, so sellers only go there when the price justifies it. That upward slope is why a burst of demand lifts price in the short run, and why the higher price eventually pulls in the extra supply that cools it back down.

Card 4 of 17

What is equilibrium price?

Equilibrium is the one price where what buyers want to buy matches what sellers want to sell.

Equilibrium price is the price where the quantity buyers want to purchase exactly matches the quantity sellers want to offer, so there’s no leftover surplus and no shortage. Graphically, it’s the point where the supply and demand curves cross, and real markets tend to drift toward this price naturally over time — useful as a baseline anytime a case asks you to reason about where a market's price should settle.

Card 5 of 17

A city regulator caps the rent on Halden Square apartments well below what the market would charge. What happens to the number of apartments available, and why?

A price held below equilibrium creates a shortage and one held above it creates a surplus.

At the capped price, the quantity renters want is larger than the quantity landlords are willing to supply, and that gap is a shortage: waiting lists, units pulled off the market, and rationing by something other than price. The mirror case is a price held above equilibrium, say a legal minimum price on a crop, where sellers offer more than buyers will take and the leftover is a surplus. Equilibrium is the only price that clears both sides, so any price fixed away from it has to show up as one of those two gaps.

Card 6 of 17

An interviewer asks whether demand for coffee has fallen when the price of coffee rises and people buy less of it. How do you answer, and why is this the classic trap?

A change in the good's own price moves you along a curve, anything else moves the whole curve.

Demand hasn't fallen, quantity demanded has. A change in the good's own price just moves you along the existing demand curve, because that curve already tells you what buyers do at every price. The curve itself only shifts when something other than that price changes, like incomes, tastes, the number of buyers, or the price of a substitute, meaning a different product that meets the same need. The same split holds on the supply side, where input costs, technology, and taxes shift the curve while the product's own price moves you along it.

Card 7 of 17

Ferrow Retail Group sells a mid-range kitchen gadget. A viral video sends its customer base sharply higher overnight, while nothing has changed about its costs or supply conditions. What happens to equilibrium price and quantity?

More demand at every price pushes both the price and the quantity sold higher.

Demand has risen while supply stayed put, so the entire demand curve shifts to the right, and the new equilibrium sits at both a higher price and a higher quantity. More shoppers now want Ferrow's gadget at every price level, so Ferrow can charge more and still sell more units — at least until supply catches up.

Card 8 of 17

What is price elasticity of demand?

Elasticity measures how sharply quantity demanded reacts when price moves.

Price elasticity of demand measures how much quantity demanded changes when price changes. Demand is “elastic” when a small price move causes a big swing in quantity, typical for goods with close substitutes, and “inelastic” when quantity barely budges even as price changes a lot, typical for necessities with few substitutes. It's a fast gut-check in any pricing case: the more substitutes a product has, the less room there is to raise price without losing volume.

Card 9 of 17

What is consumer surplus?

Consumer surplus is the extra value buyers get for paying less than they'd have accepted.

Consumer surplus is the gap between what consumers were willing to pay for a good and what they actually paid, added up across every buyer in the market. It represents the bonus value consumers walk away with, and on a graph, it’s the area between the demand curve and the market price line — worth having on hand whenever a case asks you to weigh a pricing decision's impact on customers, not just on the firm.

Card 10 of 17

What's the difference between fixed costs, variable costs, and marginal cost?

Fixed costs ignore volume, variable costs track it, and marginal cost is the cost of one more unit.

Fixed costs stay the same no matter how much you produce, like the lease on a factory or the plant manager's salary. Variable costs move with output, like raw materials, packaging, and the hours worked on the line. Marginal cost is what it costs to make one additional unit, which in the short run is essentially that unit's variable cost, since the fixed costs are already paid either way. That distinction is why a plant running below capacity will often take an order priced under its full average cost: as long as the price clears marginal cost, the order still adds something.

Card 11 of 17

The CEO of Bramwell & Co. asks you how many units of a new product line the company should manufacture to maximize profit. How would you use marginal cost and marginal revenue to find that point?

Keep producing while extra revenue beats extra cost; stop right where they meet.

Marginal cost is what it costs Bramwell to produce one more unit, and marginal revenue is what that extra unit brings in. The profit-maximizing move is to keep producing as long as marginal revenue is greater than marginal cost, and stop right at the point where the two are equal, since pushing past that point would only shrink total profit.

Card 12 of 17

What are economies of scale, and how can a company get too big for its own good?

Cost per unit falls as volume grows, until size adds enough complexity that it starts rising again.

Economies of scale means that as a firm produces more, the cost of each unit falls, because fixed costs like a plant or a software platform get spread across more units and inputs get cheaper in bulk. That's much of why scale is a genuine advantage and why large incumbents are hard to unseat. Past some point diseconomies of scale set in, as coordination slows, management layers pile up, and cost per unit climbs again. Plotting average cost against volume gives the familiar U shape, and knowing where a client sits on it decides whether growth actually helps margins.

Card 13 of 17

A private equity client is comparing two possible investments: one in an industry where hundreds of small farms sell an identical commodity grain, and one in an industry where a single firm, Bramwell & Co., owns the only patented process for a specialized industrial coating. How would you characterize each market structure, and what does that imply for pricing power?

Perfect competition has many price takers; a monopoly has one price setter.

The grain market looks like perfect competition: many firms sell identical products and no single one is big enough to move the market price, so each just accepts, or “takes,” the going price. Bramwell & Co. looks like a monopoly: it is effectively the entire market for that coating, giving it real control over price, which typically means higher prices and less output than a competitive market would deliver — and, for the PE client, a very different pricing-power story.

Card 14 of 17

NorthPeak Airlines is one of four carriers controlling nearly all the traffic on a regional route. When NorthPeak cuts fares 10%, the other three match the cut within days. What market structure is this, and why does it produce that reactive behavior?

A few big firms dominate the market, and each one watches the others' every move.

This is an oligopoly: a market controlled by a small number of large firms, where one firm’s decisions, on pricing especially, directly affect the others and usually provoke a response. Airlines are a classic real-world example of this dynamic — a handful of players control most of a route, so a fare cut by one carrier squeezes the others' volume enough that matching it is the safer move.

Card 15 of 17

A client runs one of forty coffee shops in a city, each with its own menu and its own regulars. Where does that sit among the four market structures, and how do the four compare?

The four structures run from many identical sellers to one, and pricing power grows along that line.

That's monopolistic competition: many firms competing, but each selling a slightly differentiated product, which buys a little pricing power and no more, since a rival is always a block away. Lined up, the four structures are a spectrum set by how many firms there are and how different their products are. Perfect competition has many firms selling an identical good and no pricing power, monopolistic competition many firms with differentiated goods and a little, oligopoly a handful of large firms that must watch each other, and monopoly a single firm setting price on its own.

Card 16 of 17

A client is weighing whether to enter the industrial water-filtration market, currently held by three incumbents with long-standing patents and multi-year supplier contracts. What would you tell them about barriers to entry here, and how should that shape their entry strategy?

Barriers to entry are whatever keeps new competitors from entering and challenging incumbents.

A barrier to entry is anything that makes it hard for a new firm to break into a market and compete with the companies already there — think high startup costs, patents, strong brand loyalty, or economies of scale that let incumbents produce more cheaply than a newcomer could. Here, the patents and locked-in supplier contracts are both real barriers, so the client should expect a slow, capital-intensive entry, or consider a partnership or acquisition instead of competing head-on from day one.

Card 17 of 17

Calder Foods has earned 30% margins in a plant-based snack niche for three years, and the CEO wants to assume those margins hold for another five. How would you pressure-test that?

Unusually high profits attract entrants, and without barriers new supply competes those profits away.

High margins are a signal to everyone else in the industry, so the first question is what stops a competitor from copying Calder. If entry is easy, new firms pile in, supply shifts right, price falls, and margins drift toward the level that merely compensates investors for their capital, what economists call zero economic profit. The margins hold only if something protects them: a patent, a brand that's hard to copy, a scale cost advantage, locked-up distribution, or real cost and hassle for a customer who switches. So I'd size those protections first, then stress the plan against two funded entrants arriving in year two.
