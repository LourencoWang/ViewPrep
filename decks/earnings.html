Earnings & Guidance

Four times a year every listed company reports its results, and the way the share price reacts confuses almost everyone at first. This deck explains the machinery: what consensus is, what a beat and a miss actually mean, why guidance often matters more than the numbers, how the market prices the surprise rather than the result, and what to look for in a company's statement. It is the practical half of talking about markets, the part that lets you explain why Calder Foods fell 6% on a record quarter.

Card 1 of 16

What is an earnings release, and what is in it?

The quarterly or half-yearly statement of results, with the numbers, the management commentary and the outlook.

An earnings release is the announcement a listed company puts out each quarter, or each half-year in the UK, reporting how it performed. It contains the headline numbers, revenue, profit and earnings per share, usually compared with the same period a year earlier; a commentary from the chief executive explaining what drove them; the financial statements in summary form; and an outlook or guidance section saying what management expects next. It is released before the market opens or after it closes, and is usually followed by a call where analysts question management. The share price reacts to all of it, not just the headline.

Card 2 of 16

What is consensus, and where does it come from?

The average of analysts' forecasts for a company's results, which is the number the market has already priced in.

Consensus is the average, or sometimes the median, of the forecasts published by the analysts who cover a company. If fifteen analysts expect Calder Foods to earn between 40p and 46p a share, consensus might be 43p. Because investors read those forecasts, the share price before results already reflects roughly that expectation. So results are judged not against last year but against consensus: the question is not whether the company did well but whether it did better or worse than everyone assumed. Consensus figures are collected by data providers like Bloomberg and Refinitiv, and companies often publish a compiled version themselves.

Card 3 of 16

What does it mean to beat or miss, and why does a beat not guarantee the share price rises?

A beat is a result above consensus; the price only rises if the beat is bigger than what the market had quietly hoped for.

A beat is reporting a number above consensus; a miss is below. In principle a beat is good news, but the market often expects one, especially from companies that reliably guide conservatively and then beat. If investors had been positioned for a big beat, a small one disappoints, and the price falls. The market also looks past the headline: a company can beat on earnings through a one-off tax gain while revenue misses, or beat this quarter while cutting guidance for the next. The move on the day reflects the whole package against what was already priced in, not the single number.

Card 4 of 16

What is guidance, and why does it often move the share price more than the results themselves?

Guidance is management's forecast for the coming period, and shares are priced on the future, not the quarter just finished.

Guidance is management's own forecast for the next quarter or year: a range for revenue, profit or margin, sometimes for capital spending or cash flow too. It matters more than the results because the results are history and the share price is a bet on the future. A quarter that beats by 2% while guidance for next year is cut by 10% is a bad day. Companies are careful with guidance because missing it destroys credibility, so many guide conservatively, which is why the same companies beat quarter after quarter. Reading whether guidance was raised, held or lowered is the first thing to do on results day.

Card 5 of 16

Calder Foods reports record profit, beats consensus by 3%, and the shares fall 6% by lunchtime. Explain to a client what happened.

The market prices the surprise, not the result, and a beat that was smaller than hoped, or paired with weaker guidance, is a negative surprise.

Start with what was priced in: the shares had run up 15% into results, so investors were expecting more than consensus, and a 3% beat was less than the whisper number. Then look at what else was in the statement: guidance for next year was held rather than raised, margins fell despite the profit growth, and on the call management sounded cautious about input costs. The record profit was true and already known to be likely; the new information was that growth is slowing. The price moves on new information. This is the single most useful idea in the whole topic, and interviewers love it.

Card 6 of 16

What is the whisper number?

The unofficial expectation among investors, usually higher than published consensus, that a company really has to beat.

Published consensus is the average of analyst forecasts, which can be stale because analysts update slowly and some are deliberately cautious. The whisper number is what active investors actually expect, formed from more recent data, conversations and the way the shares have traded. For a company with a habit of beating, the whisper sits above consensus, so a beat against consensus can still be a miss against the whisper, and the shares fall. There is no official source for it, which is exactly why it matters: it is the expectation that is not written down, and therefore the one that surprises.

Card 7 of 16

What is the earnings call, and what should you listen for beyond the numbers?

Management's tone, what they refuse to answer, and what the best analysts push on, which shows where the doubt is.

After the release, management hosts a call: a prepared presentation, then questions from analysts. The numbers are already public, so the value is in what is said around them. Listen for tone, whether the chief executive sounds confident or defensive; for the questions analysts ask twice, which reveal what the market is worried about; for anything management declines to quantify; and for changes in language from last quarter, a business that was growing strongly now growing solidly. Traders often move the price during the call, and transcripts are read for months afterwards. Preparing one call properly is a strong interview story.

Card 8 of 16

What are adjusted earnings, and why should you be sceptical of them?

Earnings with items management calls one-off stripped out, which is useful until the one-offs happen every year.

Companies report statutory earnings under accounting rules and, alongside them, an adjusted figure that removes items they say do not reflect the underlying business: restructuring costs, impairments, share-based pay, acquisition expenses. Sometimes that is fair, since a one-time factory closure really does distort the year. But the choice of what to exclude is management's, and companies that adjust out restructuring costs every single year are describing a permanent cost as a one-off. Compare adjusted with statutory over several years; a gap that never closes tells you the adjusted number is flattering.

Card 9 of 16

What is a profit warning, and why does the share price react so violently to one?

A statement, outside the normal calendar, that results will fall short of expectations; the market punishes the surprise and the loss of trust.

A profit warning is an unscheduled announcement that profits will be materially below what the market expects. UK rules oblige companies to issue one as soon as they know, rather than wait for results day. Shares often drop 20% or more, for two reasons. The first is the information: expectations were wrong and have to be reset. The second is credibility: management either did not see it coming or did not say so, and investors now doubt everything else they were told. There is an old market saying that profit warnings come in threes, because the problem that caused the first is rarely fixed by the second.

Card 10 of 16

What is the difference between organic growth and reported growth, and why do analysts strip out acquisitions and currency?

Organic growth is what the existing business did on its own; reported growth includes what was bought and what exchange rates did.

Reported revenue growth is the simple change from last year. Organic, or like-for-like, growth removes the effect of businesses acquired or sold during the period and of currency movements, so it shows how the underlying operations performed. Bramwell & Co. reporting 12% growth is less impressive if 8% came from a company it bought in March and 3% from a weaker pound flattering its dollar sales. Analysts and interviewers focus on organic growth because it is repeatable; acquisitions and currency are not. Retailers report the same idea as like-for-like sales, stripping out new store openings.

Card 11 of 16

Why do companies buy back their own shares around results, and how does it affect earnings per share?

Fewer shares means the same profit is spread over less, so EPS rises even if profit does not.

A buyback is a company using cash to purchase its own shares in the market and cancel them. Profit is unchanged, but it is now divided among fewer shares, so earnings per share rises. Because analysts and the press focus on EPS, buybacks can make a flat year look like a growing one, and companies know it. They are also a way of returning cash to shareholders without committing to a dividend that would be embarrassing to cut. The question to ask is whether the company is buying back because the shares are cheap or because it has run out of better uses for the money.

Card 12 of 16

What is an earnings season, and why does it matter for markets as a whole, not just individual companies?

The few weeks when most companies report; taken together, the results are the most direct reading of how the economy is doing.

Earnings season is the period, starting a couple of weeks after each quarter ends, when the bulk of listed companies report. The big US banks usually open it. Individually each result moves one stock; together they move the market, because they answer the question macro data can only approximate: are companies actually making more money? A season where most companies beat and raise guidance can lift an index even with poor economic headlines, and a season of margin pressure and cautious outlooks can sink one. Bank results in particular are read as a signal about credit and the consumer.

Card 13 of 16

How do results feed into a bank's day-to-day work, in advisory and in research?

Research analysts rewrite their models and ratings; deal teams update comps, pitch books and the story they tell clients.

In research, results day is the busiest day of the quarter: the analyst updates the forecast model within hours, publishes a note on whether the thesis has changed, and may raise or lower the rating and price target. In investment banking, every result changes the inputs to comps and precedents, so valuations in live pitches are refreshed, and a client's strong or weak quarter changes the timing advice on a sale or a capital raise. A sudden fall after a miss can also make a company a takeover target, which is where the coverage banker's phone starts ringing.

Card 14 of 16

Ferrow Retail reports a 4% revenue miss but raises full-year profit guidance. What is the market likely to do, and why?

Probably up, because guidance about the future outweighs a miss on the past, but the reason matters.

The revenue miss says sales were weaker than expected; the raised profit guidance says management expects to earn more than the market assumed for the whole year. Investors weigh the future more heavily, so the likely reaction is positive, provided the raise is credible. The detail decides it. If profit is rising because Ferrow closed loss-making stores and cut costs, the market will believe it. If it is rising because of a one-off property sale, it will not. And if the revenue miss is the start of a trend, the guidance may not survive the next quarter. Say all three and you have answered like an analyst.

Card 15 of 16

What should you read on a company before an interview at the bank that covers it?

The last two results releases, the guidance, the share price reaction, and one thing you think about it.

Read the last two earnings releases, including the outlook paragraph, and note whether guidance was raised, held or cut. Check how the shares moved on each results day and work out why, using the ideas in this deck. Skim the transcript of the latest call for the questions analysts asked twice. Then form one opinion, for example that the market is underestimating the cost savings or overestimating the growth. That takes an evening, and it lets you answer both the technical question, walk me through their results, and the fit question, why this company, with the same preparation.

Card 16 of 16

Why do analysts model a company at all if consensus already exists?

Consensus is where the market is; a model is how you decide whether the market is wrong.

A financial model is a forecast of the company's income statement, balance sheet and cash flow, built from assumptions about volumes, prices, costs and investment. Consensus is the average of many such models. The point of building your own is to have a view that differs, and to know why. If your model says Calder Foods will earn 10% more than consensus because you think its new factory ramps faster, and you turn out to be right, the shares will rise when everyone else catches up. Investing and equity research are the business of having a better forecast than consensus, which is why the model matters more than the number.
