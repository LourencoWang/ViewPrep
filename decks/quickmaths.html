Quick Maths: Banking

Banking interviews include arithmetic done out loud, without a calculator, in the vocabulary of the job: multiples, basis points, yields, leverage, per-share numbers. Nobody expects long division. They expect you to know that 20x is a 5% yield, that 25bps on £400m is £1m a year, and to chain EBITDA to a share price in four clean steps. This deck works differently from a flip deck. Every card gives you four answers and you pick one, and the wrong ones are the mistakes candidates actually make. Whichever you pick, you then get the right answer and the reasoning behind it, so a wrong choice teaches as much as a right one. The numbers are round on purpose, because the test is method and control, not speed.

Card 1 of 16

Northbay Logistics has an enterprise value of £480m and EBITDA of £60m. What multiple is that, and if a rival trades at the same multiple on £50m of EBITDA, what is the rival worth?

  • A0.125x, and £6.25m
  • B8x, and £6.25m
  • C8x, and £400m
  • D8x, and £480m

Divide EV by EBITDA for the multiple, multiply EBITDA by the multiple to get EV back.

It is 8x, and the rival is worth £400m. Enterprise value is the price of the whole business, debt and equity together, and EBITDA is operating profit before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation, a rough proxy for the cash the operations produce each year. Divide one by the other: 480 over 60 is 8. Reverse it for the rival: 8 times £50m is £400m. The £6.25m answer divides by the multiple instead of multiplying, and £480m quietly reuses Northbay’s own EBITDA. Think in units of the denominator, so £480m is ‘eight lots of £60m’.

Card 2 of 16

Ferrow Retail trades on a P/E of 20x, so investors pay £20 for every £1 of annual profit. What is the earnings yield, and why do bankers say that 20x ‘is’ 5%?

  • A0.5%
  • B5%
  • C20%
  • DIt depends on the share price

Earnings yield is one over the P/E, so a high multiple is a low yield, not a bargain.

It is 5%. The price-to-earnings ratio is the share price divided by earnings per share, so flipping it over gives the earnings yield: 1 divided by 20 is 5%, meaning each £100 of price is backed by £5 of profit a year. Answering 20% reads the multiple straight as a percentage, and you never need the share price, because the ratio already contains it. Memorise the inverses, 10x is 10%, 12.5x is 8%, 25x is 4%, 50x is 2%, because a yield compares directly with an interest rate.

Card 3 of 16

A client asks what a 25 basis point rise in the rate on their £400m loan costs them. Answer in one breath: what is the extra annual interest?

  • A£1m a year
  • B£4m a year
  • C£10m a year
  • D£100m a year

A basis point is a hundredth of a percent, so convert to a percentage before you multiply.

It is £1m a year. A basis point, written bp or bps, is one hundredth of one percent, so 100bps is 1% and 25bps is 0.25%. Bankers use them because rates move in steps too small for whole percentages to describe cleanly. Find 1% first: 1% of £400m is £4m, and a quarter of that is £1m. Stopping at that £4m is the usual slip, while £10m comes from hearing 25bps as 2.5%. Another anchor worth keeping is that 1bp on £100m is £10,000.

Card 4 of 16

How long does £1 take to double at 8% a year, and if a fund tells you its holding doubled in six years, what growth rate is it claiming?

  • AAbout 6 years, and 9% a year
  • BAbout 8 years, and 12% a year
  • CAbout 9 years, and 12% a year
  • DAbout 12.5 years, and 17% a year

Rule of 72: divide 72 by the growth rate for years to double, or by the years for the rate.

About nine years, and the fund is claiming about 12% a year. The rule of 72 says money growing at r% a year doubles in roughly 72 divided by r years, because compounding earns growth on last year’s growth. At 8% that is 72 over 8, nine years, and 1.08 to the ninth is about 2.0. Run it backwards: 72 over 6 gives about 12%, and 1.12 to the sixth is about 1.97. The 12.5 years and 17% pairing is simple interest with no compounding at all. The rule is tightest between about 4% and 15%.

Card 5 of 16

Calder Foods’ revenue has grown 10% a year for three years. A first-year analyst says that is 30% in total. What is the true figure for the three years together?

  • AAbout 21%
  • BExactly 30%
  • CAbout 31%
  • DAbout 33%

Growth compounds, so take the simple sum then add the cross terms, and the gap widens with time.

It is about 33%. Each year’s 10% lands on a bigger base, so the true total is 1.1 times 1.1 times 1.1, which is 1.331. The mental route is to start with the simple sum of 30% and add the cross terms: three years make three pairs of years, each contributing 10% of 10%, so add three lots of 1%. Adding only one of those cross terms is what gets you to 31% and stops too early. Over five years simple addition gives 50% against a true 61%, so the shortcut holds for short periods and modest rates.

Card 6 of 16

Quick one from the VP while the model loads: what is £100 arriving in two years worth today if the discount rate is 10%? Roughly, in your head.

  • AAbout £81
  • BAbout £83
  • CAbout £91
  • DAbout £121

Discounting divides by one plus the rate for each year of waiting, it does not subtract the rate.

It is about £83. Discounting is compounding in reverse: a pound next year is worth less than a pound now, because today’s pound could be invested instead, so you divide by one plus the rate for each year of waiting. Two years at 10% means dividing by 1.1 squared, which is 1.21. Round that to 1.2, and one over 1.2 is five sixths, about 0.83. Since you rounded the divisor down, the true figure is a shade lower at £82.64. The £81 answer takes 10% off twice, multiplying by 0.9 rather than dividing by 1.1.

Card 7 of 16

Halstead Foods runs a 20% operating margin. Next year revenue rises 10% and costs rise 5%. In your head, what happens to operating profit?

  • AUp 5%, profit £21
  • BUp 10%, profit £22
  • CUp 15%, profit £23
  • DUp 30%, profit £26

Grow revenue and costs separately from a base of 100, since profit is the thin slice that swings.

Profit rises 30%, to £26. Operating margin is operating profit divided by revenue, so a 20% margin on £100m of revenue means £80m of costs and £20m of profit. Grow the two lines separately: revenue becomes £110m, costs become £84m, so profit is £26m. That is operating leverage, where a small gap between revenue growth and cost growth is magnified in the thin slice left over. Answering 5%, the gap between the two rates, applies that gap to the wrong base. The new margin is 26 over 110, or 23.6%.

Card 8 of 16

Overton Digital earned £120m of net income this year and has 40m shares. Peers trade on 15x earnings. What should one Overton share be worth?

  • A£3 a share
  • B£15 a share
  • C£45 a share
  • D£1,800 a share

Net income over shares gives EPS, and EPS times a P/E gives a price per share.

It is £45 a share. Net income is the profit left for shareholders after interest and tax, and earnings per share spreads that profit across the shares: £120m over 40m is £3. A P/E multiple turns EPS into a price, so 15 times £3 is £45. The £1,800 figure is net income times the multiple, which is equity value in millions rather than a price, and you still have to divide by the share count. Run it both ways as a check: £1,800m of equity over 40m shares is the same £45.

Card 9 of 16

Kestrel Components’ shares are at £20 with 50m shares out. It has £400m of debt, £100m of cash and EBITDA of £130m. Get me to enterprise value and the multiple, fast.

  • A£700m of EV, 5.4x
  • B£1,000m of EV, 7.7x
  • C£1,300m of EV, 10.0x
  • D£1,500m of EV, 11.5x

Market cap plus net debt is enterprise value, and net debt is debt minus cash, netted in one step.

Enterprise value is £1,300m and the multiple is 10x. Market capitalisation is share price times share count, £20 times 50m, or £1,000m, and that is the shareholders’ slice alone. Enterprise value adds what the lenders are owed, because a buyer of the whole company inherits the debt, and subtracts the cash, because a buyer gets that back. Net debt is £400m less £100m, so £1,300m over £130m of EBITDA is 10x. Adding the cash instead gives £1,500m and 11.5x. Net the debt and cash first, then do one addition.

Card 10 of 16

Bramwell & Co. trades at 15x earnings and wants to buy Talbrook Industries at 10x, paying entirely in its own shares. Before any modelling, is the deal accretive or dilutive?

  • AAccretive, before any synergies
  • BAccretive, but only with synergies
  • CDilutive
  • DImpossible to say without the share counts

In an all-stock deal, a buyer P/E above the target P/E is accretive before any synergies.

It is accretive, and you can say so before a single cell is built. Accretive means earnings per share rise after the deal. In an all-share deal the buyer pays with its own stock, so compare earnings yields: Bramwell’s shares yield 1 over 15, about 6.7%, and it is buying Talbrook’s earnings, which yield 1 over 10, or 10%. Buying a 10% return with a 6.7% currency lifts EPS. If Bramwell earns £200m on 200m shares at £15 and buys £30m of earnings for £300m, EPS moves from £1.00 to about £1.05.

Card 11 of 16

A Marlstone Capital partner says the fund returned 2x in three years on one deal and 3x in five years on another. What are the two IRRs, roughly?

  • AAbout 15% and about 25%
  • BAbout 24% and about 14%
  • CAbout 26% and about 25%
  • DAbout 33% and about 40%

IRR compounds, so a doubling is worth far less a year than the gain divided by the holding period.

About 26% and about 25%. IRR, the internal rate of return, is the annual compound rate that turns the money in into the money out, so 2x in three years asks what rate, compounded three times, gives 2: about 26%, since 1.26 cubed is 2.0. And 1.25 to the fifth is about 3.05, so 3x in five years is about 25%. Answering 33% and 40% divides the gain by the years and ignores compounding. Note that a 2x taking five years instead of three falls to about 15%, which is why sponsors chase speed.

Card 12 of 16

Windmere Foods has £100m of EBITDA, £450m of debt, £50m of cash and pays £30m of interest a year. Give me leverage, net leverage and interest cover without writing anything down.

  • AGross 4.5x, net 4.0x, cover 0.3x
  • BGross 4.5x, net 4.0x, cover 3.3x
  • CGross 4.5x, net 4.5x, cover 3.3x
  • DGross 4.5x, net 5.0x, cover 3.3x

Leverage is debt over EBITDA and cover is EBITDA over interest, so on cover a bigger number is safer.

Leverage is 4.5x, net leverage 4.0x and interest cover about 3.3x. Leverage measures debt against the profit that services it: £450m over £100m means four and a half years of EBITDA would repay the lenders. Net leverage strips out cash that could repay debt today, so £400m over £100m is 4.0x, and adding the cash rather than subtracting it produces the wrong 5.0x. Cover asks about affordability instead: £100m over £30m is about 3.3x. Upside down it reads 0.3x, which is the giveaway that you have inverted it.

Card 13 of 16

A sponsor asks how much debt Halverson Industries could carry at 6x leverage on £100m of EBITDA, what that costs at an 8% rate, and whether the cover is comfortable.

  • A£600m, £8m interest, cover 12.5x
  • B£600m, £36m interest, cover 2.8x
  • C£600m, £48m interest, cover 2.1x
  • D£600m, £480m interest, cover 0.2x

Debt capacity is leverage times EBITDA, and the interest bill decides whether it can be carried.

It is £600m of debt, £48m of interest and cover of about 2.1x, which is tight. Debt capacity is the most a lender will advance against a business’s earnings, so 6x on £100m of EBITDA is £600m. Interest at 8% is £48m a year, which you can also reach as 6 times £8m, since each turn of leverage on £100m costs £8m at that rate. Charging the 8% against EBITDA instead of the debt gives the far too comfortable £8m. A 10% dip in EBITDA takes cover under 1.9x, and tax and capital expenditure come from the same pot.

Card 14 of 16

End to end, no calculator: Meridian Health has EBITDA of £80m, peers trade at 9x, it has net debt of £220m and 40m shares. What is a share worth?

  • A£12.50 a share
  • B£14.50 a share
  • C£18.00 a share
  • D£23.50 a share

EBITDA times the multiple is EV, less net debt is equity value, over the shares is the price.

It is £12.50 a share. Nine times £80m of EBITDA is £720m of enterprise value, the price of the whole business. Subtract the £220m of net debt, since that belongs to the lenders, and £500m of equity value is left for shareholders. Spread across 40m shares, that is £12.50. The £18 answer divides enterprise value straight by the share count and hands the lenders’ £5.50 a share to shareholders, while £23.50 adds net debt where it should be subtracted. Say each stage out loud with its unit.

Card 15 of 16

An associate sends you a comps sheet showing a company with £2bn of revenue and £5bn of EBITDA on a 3x multiple. Before you touch the model, what do you say?

  • AEBITDA cannot exceed revenue, so the units are wrong
  • BThe margin is unusual but possible, so flag it and move on
  • CThe multiple is the error, since 3x is far too low
  • DNothing is wrong, and 3x on that EBITDA looks cheap

EBITDA can never exceed revenue, so check units, currencies and margins before you check the maths.

The sheet is impossible. EBITDA is profit after operating costs, so it cannot be larger than revenue: £5bn on £2bn would be a 250% margin, and the likely cause is a units slip, such as £500m typed as 5,000 in a sheet stated in millions. Treating the 3x as cheap is the real error, because a multiple built on impossible inputs is a symptom rather than a bargain. Check units, then currency, then plausibility: margins mostly sit between 0% and 60% and EBITDA multiples mostly between 5x and 20x.

Card 16 of 16

Last one. When an interviewer gives you numbers to work with live, how do you actually say the arithmetic out loud so it lands well, even if you slip?

  • AWork it out silently, then give one clean final number
  • BGive the number first, and explain only if they ask
  • CQuote every digit exactly, and never round anything
  • DName the method, round out loud, then give the number

State the method, round on purpose, say which way the true answer lies, then give the number.

Name the method before touching a number, ‘EV is EBITDA times the multiple, then I take off net debt’, because a correct method with a slipped digit earns most of the credit and a right number with no method earns almost none. Round deliberately and announce it: ‘I will call 1.21 roughly 1.2, so about £83, and the true figure is a little lower’. That clause shows you know the size and direction of your own error. Calculating in silence is the worst of these, since the interviewer cannot credit a method they never heard.
