Case Maths & Mental Arithmetic

Most case interviews contain three or four calculations, none of them hard, all of them done under a stare. Candidates lose more marks on arithmetic than on frameworks, because they either go silent for a minute or come back with a number that is off by a factor of ten. This deck teaches the small set of moves that fix that: rounding sensibly, working in units that keep numbers small, percentages and growth rates, breakevens, and the habit of saying the calculation out loud as you do it. Nothing here needs more than school maths.

Card 1 of 16

Why does an interviewer care how you do arithmetic, when the job has Excel?

They are testing whether you can stay structured and calm with numbers in front of a client, not whether you can multiply.

A consultant sits in meetings where a client says a number and expects a reaction within seconds: is that big, is that plausible, what does it imply? Nobody opens a spreadsheet. The interviewer wants to see that you can take a number, decide what to do with it, do it roughly right, and say what it means, all while talking. Silence and panic are the failures, not being 3% out. So the skills are narration, sensible rounding, and sanity-checking, and a candidate who rounds 47 to 50, says so, and lands close is rated higher than one who gets 47 exactly after a long pause.

Card 2 of 16

How should you round in a case, and when should you not?

Round to one or two significant figures, say that you are doing it, and keep the direction of the error in mind.

Round aggressively but honestly. 47 million customers becomes 50 million; £3.8 becomes £4; 23% becomes a quarter. Say it as you do it, so the interviewer knows the final figure is an estimate, and note which way you rounded, so you can say the true answer is slightly lower. Do not round when the answer hinges on a small difference, such as whether a margin is 4% or 6%, or when the interviewer has given exact figures and clearly wants an exact result. If a rounded answer comes out close to a decision threshold, go back and redo it precisely.

Card 3 of 16

What is the fastest way to multiply two awkward numbers, like 340 × 25?

Break one number into pieces that are easy to multiply, or swap in a friendlier equivalent like 25 = 100 ÷ 4.

Two moves cover most cases. Decompose: 340 × 25 is 300 × 25 plus 40 × 25, which is 7,500 plus 1,000, so 8,500. Or substitute: multiplying by 25 is the same as multiplying by 100 and dividing by 4, so 34,000 ÷ 4 is 8,500. The same trick works for 50 (× 100 ÷ 2), 125 (× 1,000 ÷ 8), and 75 (× 100 × 3 ÷ 4). For something like 18 × 22, use the near-square: 20² minus 2², which is 400 minus 4, so 396. Practise these until they are reflexes and multiplication stops being where you slow down.

Card 4 of 16

How do you keep units under control when a case moves between thousands, millions and billions?

Pick one unit for the whole calculation, write it down, and convert everything into it before you multiply anything.

Most factor-of-a-thousand errors come from mixing units mid-calculation. Before you start, decide: this case is in millions of pounds. Convert every input into that unit and say so. 40 million customers times £15 a year is then 40 × 15 = £600 million, not 600 of something. When you multiply two big numbers, count the zeros separately from the digits: 300,000 × 20,000 is 3 × 2 = 6 followed by five plus four zeros, so 6 billion. Writing the unit at the top of your page and checking each result against it catches nearly every slip.

Card 5 of 16

Ferrow Retail's revenue was £820m last year and £900m this year. What is the growth rate, and how do you get it quickly?

Growth is the change divided by the starting value: £80m on £820m is a little under 10%.

Growth rate is the increase divided by the original figure. The increase is £900m minus £820m, which is £80m. Divided by £820m: 80 ÷ 820 is a bit less than 80 ÷ 800, which would be exactly 10%, so call it just under 10%, about 9.8%. Say the reasoning, because it shows you know the exact answer is slightly below the round one. The classic mistake is dividing by the new figure, 80 ÷ 900, which gives 8.9% and is the wrong ratio. Always divide by where you started.

Card 6 of 16

What is the rule of 72, and how do you use it in a case?

Divide 72 by the growth rate to get the years it takes something to double; divide by the years to get the rate.

At a steady growth rate, a quantity doubles in roughly 72 divided by the rate in years. Growing at 8% a year, revenue doubles in about nine years; at 12%, in six. It works backwards too: if a market doubled in four years, it grew at about 18% a year. In a case this lets you answer questions like what revenue will be in ten years at 7% without compounding by hand: 72 ÷ 7 is about ten, so it doubles. It is an approximation, accurate enough for rates under about 20%, and interviewers know and respect it.

Card 7 of 16

How do you calculate a percentage of a number quickly, like 17% of £340m?

Build it from 10% and 1%, or from a nearby friendly percentage, and adjust.

Ten per cent of £340m is £34m. One per cent is £3.4m, so 7% is about £24m. Add them: £58m. Or go from a friendly number: 20% is £68m, and 3% is about £10m, so 17% is about £58m. Either route takes a few seconds and can be said aloud. The same building blocks handle almost any percentage, and 5% (half of 10%), 25% (a quarter) and 33% (a third) are worth having as reflexes. If the interviewer gives you 17.3%, round to 17% first and mention it.

Card 8 of 16

What is breakeven, and how do you calculate it in a case?

Fixed costs divided by contribution per unit: the number of units you must sell to cover the costs that do not change with volume.

Breakeven is the volume at which revenue exactly covers all costs. Split costs into fixed, which do not change with volume, like rent and salaries, and variable, which do, like materials. Contribution per unit is price minus variable cost per unit, the amount each sale puts toward fixed costs. Breakeven units are fixed costs divided by contribution. If Calder Foods' new bakery costs £2m a year to run, sells loaves at £2 with £1.20 of ingredients, contribution is 80p, and breakeven is £2m ÷ £0.80, which is 2.5 million loaves. Then ask whether that is plausible against the market.

Card 9 of 16

How do you estimate a payback period, and why do clients ask for it?

Investment divided by the annual cash it generates: the years until the money is back, which is the number a nervous client wants.

Payback is the upfront cost of an investment divided by the cash it returns each year. A £6m warehouse for Northbay Logistics that saves £2m a year pays back in three years. It ignores what happens after payback and ignores the time value of money, so it is cruder than NPV, but it answers the question a client actually asks, which is how long they are exposed. In a case, calculate it, say whether three years is fast or slow for the industry, and mention that a full analysis would discount the cash flows. If savings ramp up over time, add year by year until the total passes the investment.

Card 10 of 16

Sales went from 100 to 120, then from 120 to 100. Why is the second change not the same percentage as the first?

A 20% rise followed by a 20% fall does not get you back to where you started, because the base changed.

The rise is 20 on a base of 100, which is 20%. The fall is 20 on a base of 120, which is 16.7%. Percentages are always relative to the starting point, so the same absolute move is a different percentage going up and coming down. This matters in cases about recoveries: a market that fell 50% needs to rise 100% to recover, not 50%. Interviewers use it to check whether you think about bases. When you calculate any percentage change, say the base you are using out loud.

Card 11 of 16

How do you sanity-check an answer before you say it?

Compare it with something you already know: population, GDP, a big company's revenue, a typical price.

Before you deliver a number, hold it against a benchmark. If your estimate of the UK coffee-shop market comes out at £200 billion, that is about a tenth of GDP, so something is wrong. If it comes to £5 billion, that is roughly £75 per person per year, which is plausible. Keep a few anchors in your head: the UK has about 68 million people and 28 million households; UK GDP is around £2.5 trillion; a large supermarket chain has revenue in the tens of billions. Saying the check aloud is itself impressive, and catching your own error is far better than the interviewer catching it.

Card 12 of 16

The interviewer gives you a table with revenue by product line and asks what share the top product is. How do you avoid getting lost?

Total first, then the one line you need, then divide, narrating each step.

Add the column to get the total, rounding each line as you go and saying the running sum. Then take the top line and divide it by the total. If the top product is £340m of a £1.2bn total, 340 ÷ 1,200 is a bit over a quarter, about 28%. Say what it means: one product is over a quarter of the business, so it is a concentration risk or the core to protect, depending on the case. The failure mode is trying to hold six numbers in your head; write the total down and work from the page.

Card 13 of 16

How do you handle a calculation where you realise halfway through that you made a mistake?

Say so immediately, fix it, and carry on; the recovery counts for more than the slip cost.

Interviewers expect errors, and they are watching how you handle them. The right move is to stop, say that you think you have made a mistake, identify it, and redo that step. Do not try to hide it or fudge the final number to match. A candidate who says 'that should be 60 not 600, let me correct it' has shown exactly the self-checking a firm wants. The wrong move is to carry a number you doubt through three more steps and present it. If you are unsure whether it is wrong, do the sanity check and let that decide.

Card 14 of 16

A client asks whether a 3% price rise or a 3% cost cut adds more profit. How do you answer without knowing the numbers?

Price rises usually add more, because they go straight to profit while cost cuts only apply to the cost base, which is smaller than revenue.

A 3% price rise, if volume holds, adds 3% of revenue to profit, because there is no extra cost. A 3% cut in costs adds 3% of costs to profit. Since costs are less than revenue for any profitable company, the price rise is worth more. If Bramwell & Co. has revenue of £1bn and costs of £900m, the price rise adds £30m and the cost cut adds £27m. The catch is the phrase if volume holds: price rises can lose customers, cost cuts usually do not. So the honest answer is that price is the bigger lever but the riskier one.

Card 15 of 16

How do you structure a calculation out loud so the interviewer can follow it?

Say the formula first, then the inputs, then each step, then the answer, then what it means.

Announce what you are going to calculate and how: 'Breakeven is fixed costs over contribution per unit.' State the inputs and any rounding: 'Fixed costs are £2m; contribution is £2 minus £1.20, so 80p.' Do the arithmetic in steps, each spoken: '£2m over 80p is 2.5 million units.' Give the answer with its unit, and then the implication: 'That is 2.5 million loaves a year, about 7,000 a day, which seems high for one bakery.' Five beats, always in that order. The interviewer can correct an input early, and never has to wonder what you are doing.

Card 16 of 16

What are the numbers every candidate should know cold before a case interview?

Population and households for your country, a rough GDP, working days in a year, hours in a working week, and a few everyday prices.

Have these ready: UK population about 68 million, roughly 28 million households, GDP around £2.5 trillion; US population about 335 million; the world about 8 billion. A working year is about 250 days or 2,000 hours; a year is 52 weeks and 8,760 hours. A typical UK salary is in the low £30,000s. Everyday prices for a coffee, a meal out, a phone contract, a car. Fractions as decimals: 1/8 is 12.5%, 1/6 about 17%, 1/7 about 14%, 1/3 33%. With these you can start any market-sizing or sanity check without having to guess the foundations.
