Quick Maths: Consulting

Most case interviews contain three or four calculations, none of them hard, all of them done under a stare. Candidates lose more marks on arithmetic than on frameworks, because they either go silent for a minute or come back with a number that is off by a factor of ten. This deck works differently from the others: each card gives you a question and four answers, and you pick one. Whichever you pick, the card shows which was right and walks you through the reasoning, because the wrong options are the mistakes people actually make. The moves covered are rounding sensibly, keeping units under control, percentages and growth, breakeven and contribution, and saying the calculation out loud as you do it. Nothing here needs more than school maths.

Card 1 of 16

Why does an interviewer care how you do arithmetic, when the job has Excel?

  • ATo see you stay calm and structured with a live number
  • BTo check you multiply faster than the next candidate
  • CTo see whether you have memorised the standard formulas
  • DTo find out how fast you reach for a spreadsheet

They are testing whether you stay structured and calm with a number live, not whether you can multiply.

It is the calm and the structure. A consultant sits in meetings where a client says a number and expects a reaction in seconds: is that big, is it plausible, what does it imply. Nobody opens a spreadsheet. The interviewer wants to see you take a number, decide what to do with it, do it roughly right, and say what it means, all while talking. Raw speed is not the test. Silence and panic are the failures, not being 3% out, and someone who rounds 47 to 50, says so, and lands close scores above someone who gets 47 exactly after a long pause.

Card 2 of 16

A partner drops three figures on you mid-case. How should you round them, and when should you not?

  • ANever round, since the interviewer gave you exact figures
  • BRound silently, so the answer sounds confident
  • CRound only at the very end, never mid-calculation
  • DRound hard, say that you are rounding, note which way

Round to one or two significant figures, say you are doing it, and keep the direction of the error in mind.

Round hard, out loud, and keep track of the direction. 47 million customers becomes 50 million, £3.80 becomes £4, 23% becomes a quarter. Saying it tells the interviewer the final figure is an estimate, and noting that you rounded up tells them the true answer sits a little lower. Rounding silently is the tempting move and the weak one: it reads as false precision and leaves you nowhere to go when the number is challenged. Do not round when a decision hinges on a small gap, such as a 4% margin against a 6% one, or when exact figures were handed to you on purpose.

Card 3 of 16

The interviewer says Ferrow Retail has 340 shops and each takes £25 a basket. What is 340 × 25?

  • A850
  • B6,800
  • C7,600
  • D8,500

Break one number into easy pieces, or swap in a friendlier equivalent, since 25 is just 100 divided by 4.

It is 8,500, and there are two routes to it. Decompose: 300 × 25 is 7,500 and 40 × 25 is 1,000, so 8,500. Or substitute, because multiplying by 25 is multiplying by 100 and dividing by 4, so 34,000 ÷ 4 is 8,500. The 7,600 answer comes from decomposing and then calling 40 × 25 a hundred rather than a thousand, which is exactly why you say each piece aloud; 6,800 is 340 × 20, with the 5 dropped. The same swaps handle 50 (× 100 ÷ 2), 125 (× 1,000 ÷ 8) and 75 (× 100 × 3 ÷ 4).

Card 4 of 16

The case has jumped from thousands to millions to billions. Quick: what is 300,000 × 20,000?

  • A6 million
  • B600 million
  • C6 billion
  • D60 billion

Pick one unit for the whole calculation, convert everything into it, and count the zeros separately.

It is 6 billion. Handle the digits and the zeros as two separate jobs: 3 × 2 is 6, and five zeros plus four zeros is nine, so 6 followed by nine zeros. Landing on 600 million means eight zeros were counted, and 60 billion means ten, which is the whole family of factor-of-ten errors in one place. The fix is to decide the unit before you start and say it: this case is in millions of pounds. Then 40 million customers at £15 a year is 40 × 15 = £600 million, and every result gets checked against the unit written at the top of the page.

Card 5 of 16

Ferrow Retail turned over £820m last year and £900m this year. What is the growth rate?

  • A8.9%
  • B9.8%
  • C12.5%
  • D109.8%

Growth is the change divided by where you started, never by where you ended up.

It is about 9.8%, a little under 10%. The change is £900m minus £820m, or £80m, and growth divides that by the starting figure: 80 ÷ 820. Since 80 ÷ 800 would be exactly 10%, and the real base is slightly bigger, the answer sits just below 10%, and saying that reasoning aloud is the point. The 8.9% answer comes from dividing by £900m, the figure you ended at, which is the classic slip. 109.8% is 900 ÷ 820 quoted as growth when it is a ratio, and 12.5% comes from rounding the base to 800 on both sides.

Card 6 of 16

A market has been growing 8% a year and the client asks when it doubles. Roughly how long?

  • AAbout 6 years
  • BAbout 9 years
  • CAbout 12.5 years
  • DAbout 18 years

Divide 72 by the growth rate for the years to double, or by the years to get the rate.

About nine years, because 72 ÷ 8 is 9. The rule of 72 says a quantity growing steadily at r per cent doubles in roughly 72 ÷ r years, and it runs backwards too, so a market that doubled in four years grew at about 18% a year. The 12.5-year answer treats growth as simple addition, 100% ÷ 8%, and ignores that each year lands on a bigger base than the last. Six years is the answer for 12%, and 18 for 4%. It is an approximation, close enough below about 20%, and interviewers expect you to say so.

Card 7 of 16

Calder Foods turns over £340m and the client wants 17% of it. What is the figure, and how do you build it?

  • A£5.8m
  • B£34m
  • C£58m
  • D£68m

Build any percentage out of 10% and 1%, or start from a friendly percentage and adjust back.

It is about £58m. Ten per cent of £340m is £34m and one per cent is £3.4m, so 7% is about £24m, and 34 plus 24 is 58. The other route starts friendly: 20% is £68m and 3% is roughly £10m, so 17% is about £58m. Stopping at £68m is the common slip, reaching the nearby round percentage and forgetting to come back down; £34m is 10% mistaken for the answer; £5.8m is a decimal that slid one place. Keep 5%, 25% and 33% as reflexes, and if you are given 17.3%, round to 17% and say that you have.

Card 8 of 16

Calder Foods’ new bakery costs £2m a year to run and sells loaves at £2 with £1.20 of ingredients. What is breakeven?

  • A1.0m loaves
  • B1.7m loaves
  • C2.0m loaves
  • D2.5m loaves

Breakeven is fixed costs divided by contribution per unit, which is price minus variable cost.

It is 2.5 million loaves. Fixed costs are the ones that do not move with volume, here the £2m of rent and salaries; variable costs do move, the £1.20 of ingredients in every loaf. Contribution is price minus variable cost, £2.00 less £1.20, so 80p a loaf goes toward the fixed bill, and £2m ÷ £0.80 is 2.5 million loaves. Dividing by the £2 price gives 1 million and quietly assumes the ingredients are free; dividing by the £1.20 gives 1.7 million; rounding £1.20 to £1 gives 2 million. Then ask whether 2.5 million a year, about 7,000 a day, is plausible.

Card 9 of 16

Northbay Logistics can spend £6m on a warehouse that saves £2m a year. What is the payback period?

  • AAbout 4 months
  • B3 years
  • C4 years
  • D12 years

Payback is the upfront cost divided by the annual cash it brings back, and it ignores everything after.

It is three years: £6m of investment divided by £2m of savings a year. Inverting that ratio, £2m over £6m, gives a third of a year and is the slip to watch for, so keep the unit attached, years of savings rather than a bare number. Four years comes from counting the year the money goes out as well, and 12 years from multiplying instead of dividing. Payback ignores what happens afterwards and ignores the time value of money, so it is cruder than a discounted analysis, but it answers what a nervous client actually asks: how long am I exposed.

Card 10 of 16

Sales went from 100 to 120, a rise of 20%. Then they went from 120 back to 100. What is that fall, as a percentage?

  • A16.7%
  • B18.2%
  • C20%
  • D83.3%

A percentage change is always measured against where the move started, so up and down are not symmetric.

It is 16.7%. The fall is 20 on a base of 120, and 20 ÷ 120 is one sixth. The rise was 20 on a base of 100, which is 20%, and that is the tempting answer: the same absolute move, so surely the same percentage, except the base moved underneath you. Dividing by 110, the midpoint of the two, gives 18.2% and is a different convention, not this one; 83.3% is 100 ÷ 120, a ratio rather than a change. The consequence shows up in recovery questions, since a market that fell 50% has to rise 100% to get back.

Card 11 of 16

Your sizing of the UK coffee-shop market lands on £200bn. What tells you, in one move, that it is wrong?

  • ARedo the multiplication and see whether it changes
  • BFlag that the assumptions were rough and move on
  • CHold it against UK GDP, which is about £2.5 trillion
  • DAsk the interviewer whether it sounds too big

Hold every answer against something you already know: population, GDP, a big firm’s revenue, a price.

It is the comparison with GDP. UK output is around £2.5 trillion, so £200bn of coffee would be roughly a tenth of everything the country produces, which cannot be right. A figure near £5bn passes the same test, since that is about £75 a person a year. Redoing the multiplication would not help, because the method rather than the arithmetic is what went wrong, and asking the interviewer hands them the job you were given. Keep a few anchors ready: 68 million people, 28 million households, GDP about £2.5 trillion. Saying the check out loud is itself worth marks.

Card 12 of 16

A table shows Ferrow Retail’s top product at £340m against a group total of £1.2bn. What share is that?

  • Aabout 2.8%
  • Babout 25%
  • Cabout 28%
  • Dabout 40%

Total the column first, write the total down, then divide the one line you need by it.

It is about 28%, since 340 ÷ 1,200 is a bit over a quarter. Add the column first and say the running sum, rounding each line as you go, then write the total on the page rather than holding six numbers in your head. Dividing by the other lines instead of the whole, 340 ÷ 860, gives about 40% and badly overstates the product. The 2.8% answer is a units slip, £1.2bn read as £12bn, and 25% is what you get by rounding 340 down to 300. Finish with the meaning: one product is over a quarter of the business.

Card 13 of 16

You are three steps into a calculation and realise the second step was wrong. What do you do?

  • AStop, say you have slipped, fix that step, carry on
  • BFinish the calculation, then mention it if asked
  • CAdjust the final number so everything looks consistent
  • DApologise and start the case again from the top

Say it the moment you spot it, redo that step, and carry on, because the recovery counts for more than the slip.

Stop and say so. Interviewers expect arithmetic slips and are watching what you do next, so name it, find the step, redo it and continue: that should be 60, not 600, let me correct it. One sentence like that shows exactly the self-checking a firm wants. Carrying a number you doubt through three more steps is the real damage, and quietly nudging the final figure so it looks consistent is worse, because it reads as covering up. Starting the case again throws away good work for one bad line. If you are not sure it is wrong, run the sanity check and let that decide.

Card 14 of 16

Bramwell & Co. has revenue of £1bn and costs of £900m. Which adds more profit, a 3% price rise or a 3% cost cut, and by how much?

  • AThe cost cut, by about £3m
  • BThey are the same: 3% either way
  • CThe price rise, by about £30m
  • DThe price rise, by about £3m

A price rise applies to the whole of revenue, a cost cut only to costs, which are always smaller.

The price rise wins, by about £3m. Three per cent of £1bn of revenue is £30m, and if volume holds every penny of it drops to profit, since no extra cost is incurred. Three per cent of £900m of costs is £27m. Because costs are smaller than revenue in any profitable company, the same percentage on price always beats it. Quoting £30m as the gap is the half-answer, forgetting that the cost cut still adds £27m. The catch is the phrase if volume holds: a price rise can lose customers, a cost cut usually does not, so price is the bigger lever and the riskier one.

Card 15 of 16

You have the numbers and the interviewer is watching. In what order do you say a calculation out loud?

  • AFormula, inputs, each step, answer, so what
  • BInputs, answer, formula, implication
  • CAnswer first, then the workings if they ask
  • DWork it silently, then give one clean number

Say the formula, then the inputs, then each step, then the answer with its unit, then what it means.

Formula, inputs, steps, answer, implication, always in that order. Announce the method: breakeven is fixed costs over contribution per unit. State the inputs and any rounding: fixed costs £2m, contribution is £2 minus £1.20, so 80p. Speak each step: £2m over 80p is 2.5 million units. Give the answer with its unit, then say what it means, about 7,000 loaves a day, which seems high for one bakery. Working in silence and delivering a clean number is the common instinct and the weakest route, because nobody can correct an input early or credit a method they never heard.

Card 16 of 16

Most sizings start from a headline figure. Roughly how many households are there in the UK, and what else belongs in that set?

  • AAbout 18 million
  • BAbout 28 million
  • CAbout 38 million
  • DAbout 68 million

Population, households, GDP, a working year and a few everyday prices carry most market sizings.

About 28 million. The figure it gets confused with is 68 million, the UK population, and using people where you meant households inflates a sizing by well over twice. Keep the small set cold: 68 million people, 28 million households, GDP around £2.5 trillion, the US about 335 million people, the world about 8 billion. A working year is roughly 250 days or 2,000 hours, and a year is 52 weeks. Have the fractions as decimals too, since an eighth is 12.5%, a sixth about 17% and a seventh about 14%.
