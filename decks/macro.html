Markets & Macro Awareness

Every spring week and internship interview has a moment where they ask what is going on in markets, and most first-years either freeze or recite a headline. This deck gives you the handful of ideas that connect the headlines: what interest rates actually do, why inflation is the number everyone watches, what moves a currency, and how all of it feeds through to stocks, bonds and deals. It assumes you have never read the Financial Times. By the end you will be able to hold a two-minute conversation about the economy without bluffing.

Card 1 of 18

What is a central bank's policy rate, and why does everyone care about it?

The policy rate is the price of money for the whole economy, set by a committee eight times a year.

The policy rate is the interest rate a central bank sets for the shortest-term borrowing between banks. In the UK it is Bank Rate, set by the Bank of England; in the US it is the federal funds rate, set by the Federal Reserve. Everything else prices off it. When it moves, mortgage rates, savings rates, corporate borrowing costs and government bond yields all shift, which is why a quarter-point change gets front-page coverage. In an interview you are expected to know roughly where the rate is, which way it moved last, and what the central bank said about where it goes next.

Card 2 of 18

What is inflation, how is it measured, and why do central banks target 2%?

Inflation is the rate at which prices rise, measured by a basket of goods, and 2% is the level that keeps money stable without freezing the economy.

Inflation is the rate at which the general level of prices rises over a year. It is measured with a price index, in the UK the Consumer Prices Index, which tracks the cost of a fixed basket of goods and services. Central banks target roughly 2% because a little inflation keeps wages and prices adjusting smoothly, while high inflation eats savings and makes planning much harder, and falling prices (deflation) make people delay spending. When inflation runs well above target, the central bank raises rates to slow it down; when it falls well below, it cuts to support demand.

Card 3 of 18

How does raising interest rates actually bring inflation down?

Higher rates make borrowing dearer and saving more attractive, so demand cools and prices stop rising as fast.

Raising rates works through demand. Mortgages and loans cost more, so households have less to spend; companies find projects harder to justify, so they invest and hire less; saving pays more, so money sits rather than circulates. With less spending chasing the same goods, businesses lose the ability to keep raising prices. The effect is slow, typically a year or more to work through fully, which is why central banks talk about acting before inflation has fully shown up, and why they sometimes overshoot in both directions.

Card 4 of 18

Why do higher interest rates tend to push share prices down?

A share is worth the present value of future cash, and a higher discount rate makes distant cash worth less today.

A company's shares are worth what its future cash flows are worth today, and you get from the future to today by discounting at a rate. When interest rates rise, that discount rate rises, so the same future cash is worth less now and the valuation falls. The effect is largest for companies whose profits sit far in the future, which is why fast-growing tech stocks tend to fall hardest when rates rise. There is a second channel too: when a safe government bond pays more, investors need less persuading to leave equities.

Card 5 of 18

When rates rise, which sectors tend to win and which tend to lose?

Banks usually benefit; anything valued on distant growth or bought with borrowed money usually suffers.

Banks tend to benefit because they earn the gap between what they pay on deposits and what they charge on loans, and that gap usually widens as rates rise. Insurers earn more on the money they hold. On the other side, growth stocks lose value because their profits are far in the future, property and utilities suffer because they carry a lot of debt and compete with bonds for income-seeking investors, and highly leveraged companies see interest costs eat their earnings. In an interview, one sentence on each side shows you understand the mechanism rather than the headline.

Card 6 of 18

What is GDP, and what does it mean when people say the economy is in recession?

GDP is the value of everything an economy produces in a period; a recession is that number shrinking for two quarters in a row.

Gross domestic product is the total value of goods and services produced in a country over a period, usually reported quarterly. When it grows, businesses sell more, hire more and pay more tax; when it shrinks, the reverse. The common definition of recession is two consecutive quarters of falling GDP, though economists also look at jobs and spending before calling one. For a bank, GDP matters because deal activity, loan demand and defaults all track it, so a forecast of slowing growth shows up in every conversation about pipelines.

Card 7 of 18

Why do central banks watch the jobs market so closely when they are worried about inflation?

Tight labour markets push wages up, and wages feed straight into prices.

When unemployment is low, employers compete for workers by raising pay. Higher wages are good for households but they are also a cost for businesses, who pass them on in prices, and they give people more to spend, which supports demand. That is why strong jobs data can send markets down: investors read it as a sign the central bank will keep rates higher for longer. The reverse also holds, which is why a rise in unemployment can rally bonds and, sometimes, equities, as the market anticipates cuts.

Card 8 of 18

What actually moves a currency's exchange rate?

Money flows toward higher interest rates, stronger growth and safety, and a currency rises when money flows in.

A currency is just something people buy and sell, and its price rises when more people want it. The biggest driver is interest rates: if UK rates are higher than US rates, holding pounds pays more, so money flows in and sterling tends to strengthen. Growth matters because strong economies attract investment. Risk matters because in a crisis money runs to the dollar, the Swiss franc and the yen as safe havens. Trade balances, politics and central bank comments all move the rate too, which is why FX reacts to almost every piece of news.

Card 9 of 18

Sterling falls 10% against the dollar. Calder Foods imports most of its ingredients in dollars and Bramwell & Co. sells engineering kit to American customers. What happens to each?

A weaker currency hurts importers and helps exporters, and the effect shows up in margins before it shows up in revenue.

Calder Foods now needs 10% more pounds to buy the same dollars' worth of ingredients, so its costs rise. It either raises prices, and risks losing customers, or absorbs the hit and its margin falls. Bramwell & Co. gets the mirror image: its American customers pay in dollars, and each dollar now converts into more pounds, so its revenue and margin rise without it doing anything, and it could cut its dollar prices to win share. This is why the FTSE 100, full of exporters and companies earning abroad, often rises on a weak pound while domestically focused firms fall.

Card 10 of 18

What is quantitative easing, and what is quantitative tightening?

QE is the central bank buying bonds to push long-term rates down; QT is letting them run off to push rates back up.

When the policy rate is already near zero and the economy still needs help, a central bank can buy government bonds in large quantities. That pushes bond prices up and yields down, lowers long-term borrowing costs and pumps money into the financial system. That is quantitative easing, used heavily after 2008 and in 2020. Quantitative tightening is the reverse: the central bank stops reinvesting the bonds it holds, or sells them, which drains money from the system and nudges long-term rates up. Both work on the long end of the curve, whereas the policy rate works on the short end.

Card 11 of 18

The Fed cuts rates by more than the market expected. Walk through what happens to bonds, equities, the dollar and gold that afternoon.

A surprise cut lifts bond prices, usually lifts equities, weakens the dollar and helps gold, because the surprise is what the market reprices.

Markets price what they expect, so only the surprise moves prices. A bigger cut than expected means future rates are lower than everyone assumed. Bond yields fall and bond prices rise. Equities usually rise because the discount rate fell, unless the size of the cut makes investors worry the Fed sees a recession coming, in which case the reaction can reverse. The dollar weakens because holding it now pays less. Gold rises because it pays no interest, so lower rates make it relatively more attractive. Knowing that the surprise is what matters, not the cut itself, is the thing interviewers listen for.

Card 12 of 18

What is a credit spread, and what does it tell you when spreads widen?

The spread is the extra yield a company pays over government bonds for its risk; widening spreads mean the market is getting nervous.

A credit spread is the difference between the yield on a company's bond and the yield on a government bond of the same maturity. It is the price of the risk that the company does not pay you back. When investors get nervous about the economy, they demand more compensation for that risk, so spreads widen; when they are confident, spreads tighten. Because of that, spreads are one of the cleanest gauges of market mood. A desk saying that high-yield spreads have blown out is saying that the market thinks defaults are coming.

Card 13 of 18

What do people mean by risk-on and risk-off?

Risk-on is money moving into assets that pay more but can lose more; risk-off is money running to safety.

Risk-on describes a market where investors are confident, so they sell safe assets and buy ones with higher expected returns: equities, high-yield bonds, emerging-market currencies, commodities. Risk-off is the reverse: money leaves those assets for government bonds, the dollar, the yen, the Swiss franc and gold. Most days are a mix, but a big piece of news, a bank failure or a strong jobs number, can flip the whole market into one mode. Being able to say which mode the market is in, and why, is most of what talking about markets in an interview involves.

Card 14 of 18

Oil jumps 20% in a month. Who wins, who loses, and why does the central bank care?

Energy producers win, energy users lose, and because oil feeds into the price of almost everything, the central bank sees inflation coming.

Oil producers and the countries that export it earn more for the same barrels. Airlines, shipping companies, chemicals firms and anyone with a big fuel bill see costs rise, and consumers have less to spend after filling up. Because fuel is an input to transport, plastics, food and heating, an oil spike pushes up the prices of goods across the economy, which shows up in inflation a few months later. That is why a central bank watches oil: it has to decide whether the rise is a one-off it can look through, or the start of something it needs to raise rates against.

Card 15 of 18

An interviewer asks what is going on in markets right now. How do you structure the answer?

Rates, inflation, growth, then one story you actually follow, in under two minutes.

Use a fixed structure so you never freeze. First, rates: where the central bank is and which way it is leaning. Second, inflation: the latest number and whether it is moving toward the target. Third, growth: is the economy expanding, slowing or in recession, and what the jobs market says. Then one specific story you have genuinely followed, whether a sector, a deal, a currency move or a company, with a view on why it matters. Finish with what you think happens next and why. Nobody expects you to be right; they want to see you can think in cause and effect and that you actually read.

Card 16 of 18

They ask you to talk about a recent deal or market event. How do you prepare one so it holds up to follow-up questions?

Pick one, know the numbers, know the rationale, and have a view on whether it makes sense.

Choose one deal or event from the last few months that connects to the bank or team you are interviewing with. Learn the basics cold: who bought what, for how much, how it was paid for, and the headline multiple or premium if there was one. Then the why: what the buyer says it gains, whether the market agreed on the day, and what the risks are. Finally, form an opinion, because the follow-up will be whether you would have done the deal. Two stories prepared this way beat ten headlines you can only name.

Card 17 of 18

Your team is advising Ferrow Retail on its IPO. Rates have risen, the VIX is elevated and two recent listings have traded down. Why does the macro backdrop matter for the deal, and what might you advise?

IPOs need calm markets and confident investors, so the backdrop decides whether the window is open and at what price.

An IPO asks investors to buy something new at a price set today, which they will only do when they trust prices will hold. Higher rates lower what they will pay for growth, so the valuation range comes down. The VIX, a measure of expected equity volatility, being elevated means big daily swings, and nobody wants to price a deal into that. Recent listings trading below their offer price make investors demand a bigger discount. The honest advice is often to wait for a window: a few weeks of calmer markets and a couple of successful deals. If the company cannot wait, it prices lower and sells less.

Card 18 of 18

What is the difference between monetary policy and fiscal policy, and why does the mix between them move markets?

Monetary policy is the central bank and interest rates; fiscal policy is the government and its taxes and spending.

Monetary policy is what the central bank does with interest rates and its bond holdings. Fiscal policy is what the government does with taxes, spending and borrowing. They can pull in the same direction or against each other. A government spending heavily while the central bank is trying to cool inflation means rates stay higher for longer, and more government borrowing means more bonds to sell, which pushes yields up. That combination worried markets in the UK in 2022, when a budget of unfunded tax cuts sent gilt yields sharply higher and the pound down within days. Knowing that episode is a good example to have ready.
